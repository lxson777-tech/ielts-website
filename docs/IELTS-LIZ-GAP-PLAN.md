# What IELTS Liz teaches, and what our site should add

16 September 2026

## How this plan was made

Every one of the 748 pages on ieltsliz.com was read in full and compared, section by section, with what our site teaches today. The comparison produced 441 findings, which were merged into 142 additions across eight sections. Several additions appear in more than one section; the roadmap below merges them into one list.

Two honest limits. First, only about a quarter of the findings were double-checked against our site before the usage limit; of those, 104 of 106 turned out to be genuinely missing, so the unchecked ones are very likely real too. Second, not everything Liz says matches the official IELTS rules. Where her advice is informal, outdated or contradicts the official test format or band descriptors, each section lists it under "Liz advice not to copy" with the reason, and additions that needed adjusting carry a "Caution" in the table. General Training material is excluded because our site covers Academic IELTS only.

A few claims were checked directly against our site while assembling this plan, and two recommendations were corrected as a result (see the model answers item below).

## The short version

IELTS Liz is trusted because she teaches technique before practice: exactly what each question type is testing, the traps examiners set, and how to write an answer that cannot be marked wrong. Our site is already stronger on real exam material, AI grading and live speaking practice. Where we fall short is in the teaching around that material: explaining every answer, showing what a good answer looks like, and turning mistakes into lessons.

The ten most valuable additions, in order:

1. **Model answers for the 60 real writing tasks.** The model answers page is empty. Its 57 hidden essays answer invented questions that were deleted, several of them General Training letters, so they cannot simply be relinked. New models need writing for the real tasks, which we can generate with the tools we already have.
2. **Explanations for the second half of the test bank.** Reading tests 21 to 40 and Listening tests 21 to 30 show only right or wrong, with no reasoning, evidence or transcripts. Tests 1 to 20 already have them, so the rest can be generated the same way.
3. **How to write an answer that cannot be marked wrong.** One set of rules for digits, dates, units, capitals, plurals and word limits, a short quiz, and marking that accepts every official answer form. Liz treats this as a score lever; we barely teach it.
4. **A correction to the live speaking examiner.** It currently rephrases a question in every part. Officially an examiner may only repeat the question in Parts 1 and 2 and may rephrase only in Part 3. This is a small change but it makes the practice match the real test. It needs the examiner service republished together with the site.
5. **The official band descriptors, browsable.** We already hold the official wording inside our graders and have 40 band step guides. Publishing them as pages, linked from every grading report, shows students exactly what the next band requires.
6. **Turn review into learning.** After a test, let students mark each wrong answer as a misread question or unknown English, show the split, and send them to the right lesson.
7. **Try first, then compare, in the Writing Trainer.** Students write their own overview, position or paragraph before the plan's model line appears, instead of seeing everything at once.
8. **Writing feedback that points at the mistakes.** Mark errors up inside the student's own essay, let the Task 1 grader see the chart, and warn when part of a Task 2 question was not answered.
9. **Grade the mock exam essays,** so a mock gives a true four paper band. This adds a running cost of roughly 20 US cents per mock and is your decision.
10. **Paraphrase and skill drills built from our own tests.** Synonym hunts from our reading passages and names, numbers and dictation drills from our listening audio.

## Listening

### What IELTS Liz teaches

IELTS Liz treats Listening as a test of technique, vocabulary and careful answer writing rather than raw hearing ability, and she teaches it in four repeating stages. Before the recording, students read the title, underline keywords, predict the kind of answer each gap needs from the grammar around it (a noun, a plural, a date, a name) and think of the paraphrases they are likely to hear, because the recording rarely uses the printed words. While listening, they hear it once with no pausing, use the order of the answers to keep their place, keep listening after a likely answer because speakers correct themselves, and move on at once if they miss one.

The third stage, which sets her apart, is writing the answer so it cannot be marked wrong. She drills word limits (a hyphenated word counts as one), exact spelling, singular versus plural, taking the speaker's exact words rather than a synonym, not repeating a word already printed, writing a unit only when it is missing, committing to one answer instead of brackets or alternatives, and never leaving a blank. She even runs audio-free quizzes on which written forms would be accepted.

The fourth stage, which she says matters most, is analysis. Do not open the answer key first: re-listen with the transcript, find the sentence that carried each answer, write out the paraphrase pairs, decide whether a miss was vocabulary or technique, and only then check the key, finishing by reading the transcript aloud with the audio. Her slogan is that practice is not enough and analysis is essential.

Her practice comes as short drills of 3 to 10 questions on one or two minute recordings, each tagged by question type and part, with hidden transcripts, annotated keys that explain why each wrong option fails, paraphrase maps and vocabulary lists. She also drills small skills most sites ignore: numbers, names, addresses, times, plurals and dictation. Students value her because the method is concrete, every answer is explained, and she is open about which exercises are real exam tasks and which are training.

### Liz advice not to copy

- **"All three letters must be correct to score" in list selection.** Officially each correct letter is its own numbered question worth one mark, so a partly correct set still scores. Liz's own other pages say so.
- **Band levels on single answers ("a band 6 level answer") and part targets ("aim for 90 to 100 percent on Part 1", "aimed at band 7").** Listening is scored only as a raw count out of 40 converted to a band; no band exists for one answer or one part.
- **Her score-to-band table stated as fixed fact.** IELTS publishes average marks and says the exact number varies slightly between test versions.
- **"Mainly British and Australian accents."** The official format lists British, Australian, New Zealand and North American accents.
- **"Sections" and an example answer at the start of Part 1.** Since January 2020 they are Parts, and no example is played before question 1.
- **"Answer in pencil and bring an eraser."** Since 1 May 2025 the paper test uses a black ballpoint pen supplied by the centre, and personal stationery is not allowed.
- **"Write everything in capitals" and "punctuation is ignored" as rules.** Official guidance is only that capital letters are not marked; the rest is a habit, not a published rule.
- **Two letters must be written "C F", never "C and F"; two items in one gap can be split by a space, comma or slash.** No published IELTS instruction sets either rule.
- **Missing commas in a large number could cost the mark.** There is no official penalty for unpunctuated digits.
- **"The Listening test is testing your grammar."** Grammar is not an assessed criterion in Listening; a wrong word form is wrong because it is not the word spoken or does not fit the gap.
- **"Answers always come in order" and "speakers always describe a place in a logical order" as flat rules.** It depends on the question type (as her own FAQ admits): options inside one multiple choice question are not in recording order, and map positions do not always follow the recording.
- **"A missed Part 1 answer is often repeated."** The recording is heard once and nothing is promised twice. What is true is that speakers often confirm or correct details.
- **Choosing topics because students reported them from recent real tests, and culture-specific drills such as a single country's New Year.** Real test content is confidential and never republished, and official material uses general-interest topics that need no country-specific knowledge, so this teaches topic guessing and suits only one group of students.

## Reading

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats Reading as a hunt for marks, not a comprehension exercise. Her first two principles are that you do not need to remember the passage and you do not need to understand all of it: the job is to find the few sentences that hold each answer and understand only those. Everything else follows from that. Her routine is: skim the passage in about two minutes for topic and layout only, go to the questions, name the question type and recall its technique, study the question and predict how the passage will reword it, scan for those rewordings and for words that cannot be reworded (names, numbers, technical terms), then read the sentences before and after the spot before committing. The passage's own topic word is useless as a locator because it appears everywhere.

Two skills sit under the routine: spotting paraphrase (the passage almost never repeats the question's words, and sometimes the idea is rewritten completely or carried by grammar) and picking good keywords. On top of that she teaches each question type separately, with its own rules for answer form, word limits and traps. Her True False Not Given teaching is especially detailed: False covers inaccurate as well as opposite information, Not Given means the whole meaning is missing, and each practice statement hides one named trap such as an absolute word ("only"), a swapped subject or an unstated comparison.

She is very strict about how answers are written: exact words from the passage, correct spelling, singular or plural as printed, nothing repeated from the question. She frames time as the enemy: about 20 minutes per passage, every answer worth one mark, so skip hard items and return.

Practice is split into short single type drills done untimed for learning, and full timed tests done to measure a band. What makes her valued is the explained answer key: the exact supporting quote, the paraphrase link, and why the tempting wrong answer fails. She insists practice without reviewing every mistake does not raise a score.

### Where we already match or do better

- Far more real practice: 40 complete Academic Reading tests from a publisher, single passage drills and lesson practice built from real passages. Liz has around 27 short exercises and sends students to Cambridge books for full tests.
- Real exam conditions: a 60 minute timer, passage switcher, highlighter, flag for review, question navigator and an unanswered warning. Liz's blog cannot offer this.
- Automatic scoring with a band estimate from the official Academic score table. Liz never scores anything for the student.
- Accuracy by question type, weakest first, linked to the matching lesson and drills. Liz tracks nothing.
- Table, notes and flow chart completion are taught and practised. Liz marks these as "coming soon".
- Lessons audited against the official test format, so we avoid her unofficial claims (for example, we teach the official four options in multiple choice).
- The method for each type appears inside the drill at the moment of use, not only on a lesson page.
- For tests 1 to 20, review shows the answer, an explanation and the exact supporting sentence, with a button that jumps to it in the passage.
- A live word count warning trains the word limit automatically.
- A clear study path: Reading leads the 44 lesson course and Mock Exam Day includes a full Reading sitting.
- Every test is used with publisher permission, and everything is Academic only.

### Liz advice not to copy

- **Fixed "answers come in order" rules for every type, including summary completion.** IELTS states order only for multiple choice, sentence completion, matching sentence endings and short answer, and says summary, note, table, flow chart and diagram completion answers may not follow the text. It publishes nothing on the other types.
- **"Only matching headings and choosing a title need the whole passage; everything else is pure location."** The official format describes multiple choice, matching sentence endings and summary completion as testing main ideas, and Yes No Not Given as recognising the writer's views.
- **"Choosing a title" and "list selection" as separate question types.** Neither is on the official list of question types; titles are a form of multiple choice, and shared lists belong to matching and multiple choice.
- **"You may write T, F or NG."** The official instructions say to write TRUE, FALSE or NOT GIVEN, and no official source promises abbreviations are accepted.
- **"Bring an eraser."** Test centres now supply stationery and personal pencils and erasers are not allowed in the room.
- **"Two answers in one box need both parts for one mark, and a comma is fine."** No official rule exists; on real papers these are usually two separate questions, each worth a mark.
- **"A currency symbol must be written."** There is no published IELTS rule on this.
- **Marking single questions as "band 8 to 9".** Reading has no band descriptors; a band comes only from the total raw score out of 40.
- **Rating exercises against General Training ("like General Training Section 3").** Our site is Academic only, and IELTS publishes no difficulty scale for passages.
- **Multiple choice "nearly always has three options".** The official format uses four options (A to D), plus choose two or three variants.
- **"Sentence completion is the most common question type."** IELTS publishes no such figure.
- **The band 9 candidate's tactics:** a 15 and 30 minute split between passages (the common advice, and Liz's own, is about 20 minutes each, with no extra transfer time), "go with your first instinct", reading only the first and last sentence of each paragraph for headings (officially the task tests main idea versus supporting idea, which can sit anywhere), and a simplified True False Not Given shortcut (it blurs the official difference between contradicting the text and not being in it).
- **Changing a passage word to singular in a completion answer** (her procrastination exercise). Completion answers must be copied exactly from the passage.

## Writing Task 1 (Academic reports)

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats the Academic report as a formula that can be learned, not a piece of writing invented from scratch each time. Every report has the same four-paragraph shape (introduction, overview, two body paragraphs) whatever the chart and whatever the target band, and the student adds a small technique for each visual type.

Her method runs in a fixed order. Read the task sentence and check the dates, because the dates decide the tense. Write a short factual introduction that rewords the task, keeps the chart type, dates and places unchanged, and lists the categories. Then write the overview, which she calls the most important paragraph: one separate paragraph that gathers every key feature of every chart (highest, lowest, biggest change, the exception, or for a process the number of stages and the start and end). She insists on an overview rather than a conclusion, and warns that one short sentence is usually not enough. Next, plan the body paragraphs before writing by grouping the data (things that rose together, things that fell, where group A is higher and where group B is higher, one paragraph per chart in a combined task). Support every body sentence with figures and dates, using words like "about" or "just over" when a value cannot be read exactly.

Two warnings run through everything: do not over-paraphrase (change only words you are sure of, because wrong synonyms are the main source of vocabulary errors), and show range by varying sentence structure rather than by piling up rare words.

What makes her valued is the teaching format. Almost every lesson takes a model answer apart: students reorder jumbled sentences, fill gaps, find planted grammar mistakes, fix a weak introduction, or write one paragraph before revealing the model with comments on what earned the band. She also splits chart families into useful sub-types (age-group bar charts, tables with predictions, cycles, floor plans, combined charts). Her weak spots are invented word limits, some hard "band 5" rules that the official descriptors do not state, and no marking of students' own writing.

### Liz advice not to copy

- **Word ceilings (over 210 "marked down", 215 "absolute maximum").** The official instruction sets a 150-word minimum and no maximum. The real risk is poor selection, which is judged under Task Achievement, not length itself. Her own targets also vary from page to page.
- **"Never write a conclusion."** Nothing official forbids a closing statement. The real problem is repetition and key features split across two places, and she herself allows the overview at the end.
- **"Always start the overview with Overall."** No descriptor requires that word, and repeating the same linker every time is itself a cohesion weakness.
- **Hard band gates ("no overview means band 5", "no numbers in the body means band 5", "an overview that only counts stages is band 6").** Examiners judge each criterion on best overall fit, not on single yes or no rules.
- **"No numbers or dates in the overview."** Nothing official bans figures there, and several of her own models include them.
- **"Every body sentence should be complex and over 25 words" and "simple sentences cap grammar below band 6".** Band 6 describes a mix of simple and complex sentences, and band 7 rewards variety, not uniform long sentences.
- **"Describing two pie charts one at a time will not reach band 7."** A style preference, not a descriptor. It is also the approach she recommends for combined tasks.
- **"Units are measured in ..." as a required stock sentence.** Nothing official asks for it, a pre-learned stock line earns no credit, and the wording is unnatural.
- **A fixed slot order for trend sentences.** No official rule prescribes word order, and one repeated frame works against the variety the grammar and vocabulary scales reward.
- **"Nearly every chart has one peak worth naming."** Many charts have several highs, none, or no time axis. Naming a point that is not a main feature is the mechanical detail band 5 describes.
- **"Comprised of" in her pie verb list.** A disputed form; "comprises" or "is composed of" is safe.
- **"Leave a blank line or your score may drop" and "never write the word count on your paper".** Paragraphing is marked; blank lines and word counts are not.
- **"Copied language does not count towards your band score" as a flat rule.** Examiners discount copied wording when judging vocabulary range, but the rule is not that simple.
- **Her band averaging example and self-scoring shortcuts.** They leave out the official rounding rules and are not the descriptor wording.
- **Self-assessed "band 9" labels on models.** None are officially rated; IELTS does not publish model answers. Label our own models as estimates.
- **Reported exam tasks presented as practice material.** They are rebuilt from memory and she admits some are incomplete or wrong.
- **General Training letter advice.** Correct for General Training, but our site is Academic only and letters were deliberately removed.

## Writing Task 2 (essays)

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats Task 2 as a short, repeatable routine rather than "essay writing" in general. Students first learn how it is marked: four areas worth a quarter each (Task Response, Coherence and Cohesion, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy). Her point is that only half the marks are about language, so the other half is technique that can be trained.

Preparation happens before test day. Around twenty subjects keep coming back while the wording and essay type change, so students build a bank of ideas by topic, because there is no time to invent ideas in forty minutes. The same bank also serves Speaking Part 3.

In the exam the routine is fixed. Read the instruction wording to work out which of five essay types it is (IELTS never says). Split the question into its main topic and every separate issue. Spend at least five minutes planning, including supporting points, which she names as the most common thing students skip and the main reason essays drift off topic. Then write to a set shape: a two sentence introduction (a reworded version of the question plus a clear answer that previews the main points, no hook), two or three body paragraphs with one central idea each, developed with explanation, consequence and sometimes an example, and a one or two sentence conclusion that adds nothing new. The position never changes.

Accuracy always beats ambition: reword only the words you are sure of, keep ideas simple and put the skill into the language, and avoid memorised or showy phrases.

Her practice is built on "try first, then compare": reword a question then see three models, pick the better of two introductions, fix real student sentences, critique a plan, write a conclusion then compare. She publishes over twenty complete model essays and teaches each piece of an essay separately. Students value her because the method is clear, free and consistent, although several of her firm rules are her own conventions rather than official ones.

- **"Avoid semicolons and colons, just use commas and full stops".** The grammar criterion rewards a range of correctly used punctuation, so avoidance is not a rule.
- **"Do not use examples from your personal life".** The printed Task 2 instructions invite examples from your own knowledge or experience.

## Speaking

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats Speaking as a showcase of language, not a test of content. Her most repeated message is that the examiner marks four language criteria (Fluency and Coherence, Vocabulary, Grammar, Pronunciation) across the whole test, so knowledge, opinions, body language and appearance earn nothing. She uses that one idea to settle dozens of student worries: having no opinion, misreading the card, disliking the topic, or meeting an examiner who does not smile.

From there she builds a preparation system rather than a set of scripts. Students gather recycled topics, prepare ideas and topic vocabulary for each one (never memorised answers), then practise aloud: record a handful of questions on a phone, never write the answers out, listen back and check length, fillers, silences, repeated words, tense range and pronunciation, fix the weakest point and record again.

Technique is taught part by part. In Part 1 you give a direct answer that mirrors the question's grammar ("Yes, I can"), match its tense, add a little more and keep talking until the examiner moves on. She teaches Part 1 as around 15 recurring question forms that apply to any topic, and stresses that Work, Study, Home or Hometown always come first. In Part 2 you check the subject fits the card, plan language as well as ideas during the minute (a past memory, a future hope, a conditional), paraphrase the card in your first line, treat the bullets as prompts rather than a checklist and use named moves to fill two minutes. Part 3 is a discussion about people and society, with a different answer shape for each question type and an examiner who may push back.

What makes her valued is the confidence layer: exactly what the examiner may and may not do in each part, what you may ask, and a scripted way out of every breakdown. She teaches through small reveal quizzes where students guess first, and many models come with audio and a note on what earns the score.

### Liz advice not to copy

- **"Each criterion is worth 25 percent."** The official Speaking band is the average of four whole band scores, reported in whole and half bands. A criterion is not a percentage and rounding matters.
- **"Using wanna and gonna will raise your pronunciation score."** No descriptor gives credit for slang reductions, and forms like "dunno" or "coz" can count against vocabulary style.
- **"You could get band 9 using only 'and'; a range of linkers is not rewarded."** The band 7 Fluency and Coherence descriptor asks for a range of connectives and discourse markers used with some flexibility.
- **"The whole test is informal in all three parts."** IELTS describes it as a conversation, not as informal. Part 3 is discursive and Lexical Resource assesses appropriate style.
- **"Going off topic never costs anything."** A talk swapped for a different or memorised subject can be discounted, and development and coherence are assessed.
- **"You can lie and it will not affect your score."** This is not an official position, and invented stories tend to sound rehearsed. Teach "adapt the truth" instead, and never anything untruthful at the ID check.
- **"The examiner will not move on before one minute of your talk."** Not a published rule. The official instruction is a talk of one to two minutes, stopped at two.
- **"Each question tests one grammar point and the examiner stops you once you use it."** Examiner questions are not published as targeting named structures.
- **"Part 1 always has exactly 12 questions across 3 topics."** The number of topics and questions varies.
- **"A heavy accent makes your score drop drastically."** The descriptors grade how easy you are to understand on a scale. An accent on its own is not penalised.
- **Predicted topic lists for a given year.** IELTS does not publish or announce Speaking topics in advance, so these are crowdsourced guesses.
- **Self awarded "band 9" labels on model answers.** They are not officially rated. Our models should keep honest, calibrated band labels.
- **"Don't choose work or study for an achievement card" and "'cost a fortune' is better than 'cost an arm and a leg'".** Personal preferences with no basis in the marking criteria.
- **Her climate change model listing earthquakes and tsunamis as effects.** A factual error. Check the facts in any model we write.
- **A full prep pack on riots and political protests.** She also says IELTS avoids political topics, and official practice supports that. Do not build material around them.

## Vocabulary and grammar

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats vocabulary and grammar as one scored resource, and she teaches it backwards from the marking criteria: vocabulary is a quarter of the Writing and Speaking mark, so every lesson opens by saying which marks it protects. Her central message is that accuracy beats ambition. A plain word used correctly, spelled correctly and in the right register scores better than an impressive word used wrongly, so students must learn how a word behaves (its word forms, the preposition and article it needs, whether it is countable, and whether it is formal or informal), not only what it means.

Grammar is taught inside real exam sentences rather than as a separate textbook. Linking words come with the sentence pattern that must follow them ("despite" plus a noun, "although" plus a full clause, "in order to" plus a verb), and her quizzes use wrong answers that have the right meaning but the wrong grammar. Articles are taught through crime and country sentences, prepositions through Task 1 trend sentences ("rose by 5,000 from 20,000 to 25,000"), and word forms through error correction.

Her paraphrasing advice is distinctive and cautious: use three techniques (synonyms, changing the word form, reordering the sentence), but do not paraphrase everything. Keeping the topic word is fine even at high bands, and forced synonyms are the typical band 6 trap.

Each topic page follows a fixed pattern: word forms and common errors, grouped word lists with plain definitions, audio for pronunciation, a short exercise whose answer key explains why the tempting wrong answer fails, then the words shown working inside a model paragraph or speaking answer, with links to matching essay questions. She also covers practical detail few teachers do: Listening spelling drills with letters and place names, UK versus US spelling consistency, approximation language for chart figures, and labelling every expression as safe for writing or speaking only. Students value her because the advice is careful, concrete and built around avoiding mistakes. Her weak points are keys missing from many exercises and some personal rules stated as if they were official.

### Liz advice not to copy

- **"Never start a sentence with But or Because."** This is a style habit, not a grammar rule or a band descriptor requirement. "Because" clauses at the start of a sentence are correct formal English, and the descriptors reward a range of structures.
- **"The examiner looks for sentences that begin with an -ing word."** No official descriptor names a structure examiners look for, and teaching one encourages formulaic writing.
- **"Mechanical use of Firstly and Secondly holds you below band 7."** The official descriptor describes over-use as a weakness to judge, not a fixed cap.
- **"Linking words are 25 percent of the mark."** That quarter is the whole Coherence and Cohesion criterion, which also covers paragraphing and logical order.
- **"A memorised answer could get band 0."** Memorised language is penalised, but IELTS does not publish that specific score.
- **Fixed word ranges (170 to 190 for Task 1, 270 to 290 for Task 2, nothing over 300) and per-paragraph word budgets.** IELTS publishes only the minimums and no upper limit; these are Liz's own targets.
- **"The under-length penalty has been removed."** This reports internal examiner instructions that cannot be checked against published IELTS material.
- **"Copied question words are not counted, so you may fall under the word count."** The mechanism is not a published counting rule; the safe message is simply that copied wording is not your own language, so paraphrase.
- **"Do not suggest a law that already exists."** Examiners are not subject experts and do not mark real-world accuracy of proposals; they mark relevance, position and development.
- **"A single expression such as lingua franca is high band vocabulary."** Bands come from overall range and accuracy, not showcase phrases.
- **Anything aimed at General Training** (for example linking-word lists described as serving General Training letters). Our site is Academic only.

## Band scores, test information and test-day tips

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats IELTS as a rules-based exam, not an English course. Her whole method rests on one idea: your band score comes from two separate things, your real level of English and your knowledge of the test. ### Liz advice not to copy

- **Fixed Listening score boundaries** (23 is band 6, 30 is 7, 35 is 8). Official conversions are indicative and vary slightly between test versions, so a student near a boundary can be misled. Keep our caveat.
- **Answer formatting habits taught as marking rules** (for example 9.30am is right and 9:30 is wrong, never write "of" in a date). No official source says so and Cambridge answer keys are more permissive. Teach them only as safe habits.
- **"You will be marked down for capitals"** and her contradictory advice on capitals. Capitals are not penalised in Listening and Reading. In Writing, punctuation is judged under grammar, but no official rule bans or scores capitals as she describes.
- **Pens versus pencils.** Her pages say both "only a black pen" and "pencil is required". Check with the official source and the test centre and publish one answer.
- **"Take your toilet break in Reading" as a firm rule.** Many centres do not let you leave in the last 10 minutes of a section and any break costs exam time. Present it as the least costly option, subject to centre rules.
- **IELTS Online described without its limits.** It is not accepted by every organisation and is not available for UK visa purposes.
- **"Speaking is always face to face."** The online version runs Speaking by video call, which her own site describes elsewhere.
- **Computer test quirks stated as fact** (the timer hiding seconds, an example conversation being played). Her own candidates report these differently. Only publish what official sample tests confirm.
- **Headline stories of band 9 in 5 or 10 days.** Those candidates already had a high level of English, so the stories teach students that a high band is a two-week project.
- **The remark story with a 1.5 band rise as a normal outcome.** Her own page says half a band is typical and one band is rare.

## Skills exercises and everything else

### What IELTS Liz teaches

Liz treats IELTS as a test you beat by understanding it first, drilling small skills second, and doing full exam practice a distant third. She says most students underperform because they misunderstand the rules and marking, not because their English is weak, so a large part of her site simply explains what is marked, what is not, and how the day works.

Her exercises are tiny and repeatable, each aimed at one skill: rewrite only the first sentence of an essay, combine short sentences into one, punctuate a run on paragraph, spot article mistakes, find synonyms in a short passage, or write down a short recording word by word and then sort your own errors by type. Her paraphrasing teaching is unusually precise. Where no safe synonym exists, give examples instead.

She teaches heavily from flawed work: a weak answer, a numbered list of its exact faults, then a better version, often with a "safe" rewrite and a riskier "ambitious" one with a warning attached. ### Liz advice not to copy

- **General Training letters (complaint, apology, invitation, thank you, letters to authorities or editors)**, including her letter rules (170 to 190 words, one bullet per paragraph, never write the word count). Our site is Academic only by choice. Instead, say plainly on the site that it is Academic only, and remove the two leftover letter models and the "reports and letters" heading.
- **"Reading answers always follow passage order."** False for matching headings, matching information and matching features; her own answer key breaks it.
- **Her raw score to band table as fixed fact.** Boundaries vary by test version.
- **The "around 100 USD in 2014" re marking fee** and other dated admin facts. Fees are set locally and have changed; her site is updated only occasionally.
- **"You cannot use a highlighter in reading"** stated without saying it applies to paper only, and **"paper and computer tests are identical"**. Both contradict her own pages.
- **"Your ideas are not marked in Speaking"** stated baldly. Undeveloped or off topic answers still cost marks under Fluency and Coherence.
- **Her error count ladder (frequent errors band 5, few errors band 7).** Her own shorthand; the official descriptors also judge range.
- **Invented cut offs and benchmarks** ("under 70 percent, study the test first", "a band 7 student spots 6 of 7"). No calibration behind them.
- **Word counts as rules** (introductions of 50 to 60 words, paragraph lengths). IELTS only sets minimum lengths.
- **"Do not mix British and American spelling."** Examiner folklore; the descriptors penalise spelling errors, not mixing.
- **Absolute bans on single synonyms** (never "attire", never "performers") presented as marking rules. They are her judgement calls.
- **"Few people means not enough people."** Imprecise; "few" means a small number with a negative feel.
- **"Topics are chosen from trending news"** and **"examiners are mostly British or Australian".** Unsupported and likely wrong.
- **Self assigned band labels presented as certified** (her "band 9" letters and "band 6.5" essay). Useful estimates, not official ratings.
- **Crowd sourced question year tags treated as what was actually set.** They show only what candidates remembered.
- **A study partner matching board.** Our live voice examiner already fills the need for a speaking partner, so say that instead of building one.
