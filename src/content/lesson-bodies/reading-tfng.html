True / False / Not Given

True, False, Not Given

What is it?

The official name for this task is True / False / Not Given. On the paper the rubric reads: "Do the following statements agree with the information given in Reading Passage 1?" You read a statement and decide whether the passage confirms it (TRUE), contradicts it (FALSE), or simply doesn't mention it at all (NOT GIVEN). The key is that NOT GIVEN means the passage is silent. Not that the statement is wrong. This task is always about factual information in the text, never about the writer's opinions: that is the separate Yes / No / Not Given task.

Key Distinctions

  • TRUE: the statement matches the passage information
  • FALSE: the statement directly contradicts the passage
  • NOT GIVEN: there is no information to decide either way
  • Write TRUE, FALSE or NOT GIVEN. Writing YES or NO on this task is marked wrong, even if the thinking behind it was right
  • In practice these answers usually appear in the order of the passage, but IELTS does not publish this as a rule. Use it as a guide for where to start looking and never as proof of an answer
  • A NOT GIVEN answer has no location in the passage at all, so the order guide cannot help you find one. Never decide a statement is TRUE or FALSE simply because it "should" be at that point in the text

How to Approach It

  1. Read each statement carefully and identify keywords.
  2. Scan the passage for the part that deals with that statement. These answers usually run in passage order, so starting near the last one you found saves time, but treat that as a guide only.
  3. Read that section and the sentences around it. Not just one line.
  4. Focus on meaning, not just word-matching. The passage will paraphrase the statement.
  5. Be especially careful with NOT GIVEN. Ask: "does the passage give any information about this at all?"
⚠ Trap: "believed" ≠ factual truth ⚠ Trap: numbers without context (increase? decrease?) ⚠ Trap: idiomatic expressions. Read for meaning

The Decision Test

Almost every mark lost on this task is lost by deciding with a feeling: "hmm, that sounds about right." Feelings are not repeatable, so they cannot be trained. Replace the feeling with one short test that you run on every single statement, in this order.

  1. Read the statement and say it back to yourself in your own words.
  2. Before you go near the passage, write down (or say clearly in your head) exactly what the passage would have to say for this statement to be FALSE.
  3. Now go and look for exactly that, and nothing else.

Then decide using these three lines and no others:

  • The passage says the FALSE sentence you wrote: the answer is FALSE.
  • The passage says the statement itself, in whatever words it chooses: the answer is TRUE.
  • The passage says neither of those two things: the answer is NOT GIVEN.

The value of writing the FALSE sentence first is that it turns a vague search ("is this in here somewhere?") into a search for one specific thing. You know what you are hunting before you start, so you stop when you find it and you stop when you have proved it is not there.

✓ Write the FALSE sentence before you search ✓ Hunt for that one sentence only ✓ Let the three lines above choose the answer

What FALSE Really Covers

FALSE is not only the exact opposite of the statement. It also covers anything the passage shows to be inaccurate: a different date, a different number, a different place, a different person, a different reason.

If the passage says a bridge opened in 1923 and the statement says it opened in 1932, the answer is FALSE. The passage has told you the statement is wrong, and that is enough. You do not need the passage to say "it did not open in 1932" in so many words, and you should not drift towards NOT GIVEN just because the wording is not a neat mirror image.

What NOT GIVEN Really Means

NOT GIVEN means the whole meaning of the statement is missing from the passage. That is the only thing it means.

It does not mean the statement sounds unlikely. It does not mean you personally doubt it, and it does not mean the statement feels like too big a claim. If you catch yourself arguing in your head about whether something is believable, you have stopped reading the passage and started guessing. Go back to the page and ask one question instead: is this idea here at all?

The decision test on three statements (full worked example)
Passage extract

The Halden Bridge was completed in 1923. It was designed by the engineer Marta Lind, and the money for its construction came from a group of local merchants. Within ten years the bridge was carrying more traffic than any other crossing in the region, and a second deck was added in 1968 to ease the congestion.

Statement 1

"Marta Lind paid for the construction of the Halden Bridge."

  • What would make this FALSE: the passage saying the money came from somebody other than Lind.
  • What the passage says: "the money for its construction came from a group of local merchants".
  • Decision: that is the FALSE sentence, word for word in meaning. The answer is FALSE.

Why it is tempting: Lind is in the passage, and she is attached to the bridge. But she is attached to the design, not to the money.

Statement 2

"By 1933 no other crossing in the region carried more traffic than the Halden Bridge."

  • What would make this FALSE: the passage saying that some other crossing was busier at that time.
  • What the passage says: the bridge was completed in 1923, and "within ten years" it was carrying more traffic than any other crossing. Ten years after 1923 is 1933.
  • Decision: the FALSE sentence is not there, and the statement itself is, in different words. The answer is TRUE.

Note that the date 1933 never appears. You had to do the arithmetic yourself, which is normal. Arithmetic on the passage's own numbers is still the passage's information.

Statement 3

"The second deck reduced the number of accidents on the bridge."

  • What would make this FALSE: the passage saying that accidents stayed the same, or rose, after 1968.
  • What the passage says: the deck was added to ease congestion. Accidents are never mentioned, in any form.
  • Decision: neither sentence is in the passage. The answer is NOT GIVEN.

Why it is tempting: less congestion probably does mean fewer accidents. But that is your reasoning, not the writer's, and you are only ever marking the writer's.

The Trap List

These statements are built on purpose, and the same dozen or so tricks come round again and again. Learn to recognise them so you know where to read hard. But recognising a trap is not an answer on its own: it tells you where to look, and the decision test above still decides.

TrapWhat it looks like, in one line
Absolute words
only, all, never, the first		 Passage: "many early lamps burned whale oil." Statement: "all early lamps burned whale oil."
Widened or narrowed scope Passage: the custom survives in rural Portugal. Statement: the custom survives across Europe.
Swapped subject
right fact, wrong person or thing		 Passage: Lind designed the bridge and Ruiz surveyed the ground. Statement: Ruiz designed the bridge.
Who does the action Passage: the city council funded the library. Statement: the city council built the library.
Unstated cause Passage: the mill closed in 1990, and the town lost a third of its people during the 1990s. Statement: the mill's closure caused the town to lose people. Both facts are there, but the link between them is not.
Unstated comparison Passage: describes the Vela river, then describes the Oren river. Statement: the Vela is longer than the Oren. The passage never puts them side by side.
A number attached to a different thing Passage: 300 people attended the opening lecture. Statement: the course has 300 students.
A belief presented as fact Passage: nineteenth century doctors believed the illness travelled through the air. Statement: the illness travelled through the air. "They believed it" is not "it is so".
Time expressions Passage: the ban has been in force since 2010. Statement: the ban was in force for ten years before 2010. The same year, the opposite decade.
"More than" and "up to" Passage: up to 50 pairs of birds nest on the island. Statement: 50 pairs of birds nest on the island. "Up to 50" is equally satisfied by 12.
A plan or a hope treated as a fact Passage: the company hoped to open ten shops by 2020. Statement: the company opened ten shops by 2020. Wanting to is not doing.
A word the passage never touches Passage: explains in detail how the roof was built. Statement: the roof was expensive. Cost is simply not in the text. This is the classic NOT GIVEN.
A near word that is not the same word Passage: the tickets became cheaper. Statement: the tickets became free. In the same family, "rarely" is not "never" and "reduced" is not "stopped".
⚠ One warning that covers the whole list above. None of these traps carries an automatic answer. An absolute word such as "all" is not proof of FALSE, and an unfamiliar word is not proof of NOT GIVEN. The passage contradicting a statement and the passage never mentioning it are two completely different situations, and only the passage in front of you can tell you which one you are in.
