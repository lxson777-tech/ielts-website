How Reading actually works

The mindset

Your job is not to understand the passage. Your job is to find the few sentences that hold each answer, decide what they say, and move on. Everything else in the passage can stay unread.

Nobody reads all three passages properly in 60 minutes, and nobody is expected to. The test is 40 separate hunts, not one long read. Students who try to read every word first run out of time and then rush the questions, which is where most marks are lost.

Every answer is in the passage. You are never asked to bring in your own knowledge, and you are never asked to remember anything, because the text stays in front of you the whole time.

The routine behind every question type

Each question type has its own tricks, but they all sit on top of the same six steps. Learn these once and each new type is a small variation.

  1. Skim the passage for about two minutes. You only want the shape of it: what each paragraph is roughly about.
  2. Read the question and decide exactly what it is asking for, and what form the answer must take.
  3. Predict the rewording. The passage will almost never use the same words as the question, so ask yourself how the writer would have said it.
  4. Scan for that reworded idea, or for something that cannot easily be reworded, such as a name, a year, a number or a place.
  5. Read around the place you found, carefully and slowly. This is the only slow reading you do, and it is usually two or three sentences.
  6. Answer and move on. Do not reread to reassure yourself. Never leave a blank, because a wrong guess costs nothing.

The skip and come back trick For any type whose answers run down the passage in order, being stuck is much less painful than it feels. If question 4 will not come, leave it and do question 5. The answer to 4 is almost always somewhere between the spot where you found 3 and the spot where you found 5, so when you come back you are not searching the passage any more, you are searching a short stretch of it. That turns a hopeless hunt into a ten second one.
⚠ This trick does not work for Not Given answers. A Not Given answer is not anywhere in the passage, so there is no stretch of text to narrow down to. If you have squeezed the gap between two answers and still find nothing about the statement, that emptiness is the answer

How to train, in order

Reading improves in a fixed order, and doing these steps out of order wastes time. Timed tests at the start only tell you what you already know, that you are too slow.

  1. Paraphrase and vocabulary first. If you cannot see that "a sharp fall in numbers" and "the population declined steeply" are the same idea, no technique will help you. Start with the Spotting Paraphrase lesson below.
  2. Then the question types, one at a time. Take one type, learn what it actually asks, and do only that type until it stops surprising you. Then take the next one. Mixing types while you are still learning them teaches you nothing about either.
  3. Then speed at finding answers. Once the types are familiar, practise the hunt itself: how fast can you locate the sentence, without worrying about the whole passage.
  4. Then full passages with no clock. Do a whole passage properly, taking as long as you need, and check every answer against the text afterwards so you can see where you went wrong.
  5. Only then, timed tests. Now the clock is measuring a real skill rather than punishing you. Score every test out of 40 and track it.
About the Test

IELTS Reading at a Glance

The Basics

  • Total time: 60 minutes: unlike Listening, there is no extra transfer time
  • 40 questions across 3 passages, 1 mark each
  • Around 2,150-2,750 words of text in total
  • Passages generally increase in difficulty from Passage 1 to Passage 3
  • No negative marking. Never leave a blank, always guess
  • Spelling and grammar count: a misspelled answer is marked wrong
  • In gap-fill tasks a hyphenated word such as "check-in" counts as one word, and a number counts as one word. Contracted words such as "they're" are never tested

The Three Passages

  • Passage 1. Usually the most straightforward. Often descriptive or factual (e.g. a process, a place, an invention). Aim for speed here.
  • Passage 2. Medium. Often more analytical or discursive, on a topic of general academic interest, with heavier paraphrasing between question and text.
  • Passage 3. Usually the hardest. At least one of the three passages contains a detailed logical argument. In practice that is normally Passage 3, which is where opinion-based questions such as Yes / No / Not Given appear.
  • Budget roughly 20 minutes per passage, then move on. The paper itself prints "You should spend about 20 minutes on this passage and its questions", but that is advice, not a rule: the 60 minutes covers all three passages together and nothing stops you moving between them. The easy marks in a later passage are worth more than one stubborn question.
  • Texts come from books, journals, magazines, newspapers and online resources, written for a non-specialist audience. They range from descriptive and factual to discursive and argumentative. No specialist knowledge is needed; every answer is in the text.
