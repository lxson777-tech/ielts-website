Summary, Note, Table & Flow-chart Completion

What is it?

This is one family of task with four possible layouts. A summary is a short paragraph retelling part of the passage with gaps. Notes present the same idea as a bullet-point outline, useful when the passage lists several separate points rather than telling a continuous story. A table arranges the missing information into rows and columns, usually when the passage compares several items against the same set of features. A flow-chart shows a process as a sequence of boxes joined by arrows, used when the passage describes steps that happen one after another. Whichever layout appears, the skill is identical: find the missing word for each numbered gap.

Two Variants

  • Variant 1, words from the passage: "Complete the summary below. Choose NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS from the passage for each answer." You must copy exact words.
  • Variant 2, choosing from a box: "Choose your answers from the box below and write the correct letter, A-J." You select from a fixed list of options rather than writing your own words. The words in the box are usually paraphrases rather than the passage's exact wording, and some options are distractors that are never used.
  • The answers may not come in the same order as the passage, but they usually all come from one part of it rather than the whole text
  • In Variant 1 the word limit is printed with the task. A hyphenated word counts as one word, and a number counts as one word

How to Approach It

  1. Read the whole summary, notes, table, or flow-chart first, ignoring the gaps, to understand what part of the passage it retells.
  2. For each gap, decide what kind of word is missing (a noun, a number, a process, a name?) from the surrounding text.
  3. Find the part of the passage the task is drawn from. The answers will usually all sit inside that one part, though not necessarily in the order of the gaps.
  4. In Variant 1, copy the exact word(s) from the passage. In Variant 2, compare each remaining box option carefully. More than one may look tempting.
  5. Reread the completed sentence or step to check it makes sense grammatically and matches the passage's meaning.
⚠ Trap: writing a paraphrase instead of the passage's exact word in Variant 1 ⚠ Trap: choosing a box option that repeats passage words but changes the meaning ⚠ Trap: going over the stated word limit
Example passage: Urban Beekeeping

Once confined to rural farms, beekeeping has spread rapidly into cities over the past two decades. Rooftops, balconies and unused areas of parks in cities such as London, New York and Tokyo now host thousands of hives, tended by hobbyists and professional apiarists alike. Supporters point out that cities can actually offer bees a more varied diet than intensively farmed countryside, since gardens, street trees and even weeds growing in pavement cracks provide a wide range of pollen throughout the year, whereas a single crop such as wheat or oilseed rape flowers for only a few weeks.

Urban beekeeping has also become a popular tool for environmental education. Many schools and community centres now keep an observation hive, allowing children to watch bees at work through a glass panel without disturbing the colony. Local councils have started to offer small grants to residents willing to install a hive, partly to boost pollination of public gardens and partly to raise awareness of declining bee populations worldwide.

However, some ecologists warn that the trend has grown faster than the evidence supporting it. Introducing large numbers of honeybees into a city can increase competition for nectar with wild, native bee species, several of which are already struggling to survive. A handful of studies have found that in areas with the highest density of urban hives, sightings of wild bumblebees fell noticeably within a few years, suggesting that a well-intentioned solution may be creating a new problem of its own.
Example summary (words taken from the passage): Beekeeping used to happen only in the countryside, but it has now become common in (1) areas. Cities can give bees a (2) diet, because many different plants flower throughout the year. Hives are also used in schools as a form of (3), letting children observe bees safely. Some ecologists worry, however, that too many honeybees compete with (4) species for food, which may explain a drop in (5) sightings near areas with many hives.
