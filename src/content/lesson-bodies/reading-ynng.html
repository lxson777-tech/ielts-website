Yes, No, Not Given

What is it?

You read a statement and decide whether it agrees with the writer's view or claim (YES), contradicts it (NO), or whether the writer never expresses a view on it (NOT GIVEN). Officially this is called Identifying the writer's views or claims, and it is a different question type from True / False / Not Given.

Key Distinctions

  • YES: the writer expresses this opinion, or clearly implies it
  • NO: the writer expresses the opposite opinion
  • NOT GIVEN: the writer never takes a position on this point
The one thing that separates this from True / False / Not Given True / False / Not Given asks about facts stated in the passage. Yes / No / Not Given asks about what the writer thinks. So a statement can be perfectly true in the real world and still be NO, because the writer argues against it. Your own knowledge and your own opinion are irrelevant. Only the writer's position counts.

How to Approach It

  1. Expect these in Passage 3, in argumentative or opinion-based texts. Passage 1 is rarely opinionated enough to support them.
  2. Locate the relevant part of the passage. Answers appear in passage order.
  3. Hunt for opinion language, not facts: arguably, surely, it is regrettable that, critics overlook, the real question is, too often.
  4. Ask "does the writer agree with this statement?" rather than "is this statement true?"
  5. If the writer only reports someone else's opinion without endorsing it, that is usually NOT GIVEN for the writer's own view.
  6. Watch the strength of the claim. A writer who says a policy "has some merit" has not said it is "the best solution".
⚠ Trap: reporting an opinion is not holding it ⚠ Trap: "many argue that…" is other people, not the writer ⚠ Trap: overstated statements ("all", "never", "the only")
Passage: The Case Against Open-Plan Offices

The open-plan office was sold to a generation of managers as an engine of collaboration. Remove the walls, the argument ran, and ideas would flow freely between colleagues who had previously been sealed off from one another. It is a seductive picture, and it is largely unsupported by the evidence. When researchers at Harvard tracked employees before and after a move to an open floor, face-to-face interaction fell by roughly seventy per cent. Staff did not talk more; they retreated behind headphones and moved their conversations to email.

Defenders of the format point out that it costs far less to seat a workforce without partitions, and on that narrow point they are correct. What they rarely acknowledge is the bill that arrives later, in the form of lost concentration. Some studies have attempted to quantify the productivity cost, though the figures vary too widely to be relied upon. My own concern is different, and simpler: we redesigned the places where people spend most of their waking hours around a claim about human behaviour that nobody had properly tested. That the claim turned out to be wrong is unfortunate. That it was adopted so enthusiastically, and questioned so late, is harder to forgive.
