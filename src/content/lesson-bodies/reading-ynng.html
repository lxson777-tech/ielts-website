Yes / No / Not Given

What is it?

The official name for this task is Yes / No / Not Given. On the paper it reads something like: "Do the following statements agree with the claims of the writer in Reading Passage 3? Write YES if the statement agrees with the claims of the writer, NO if the statement contradicts the claims of the writer, NOT GIVEN if it is impossible to say what the writer thinks about this." Some versions ask about the views of the writer rather than the claims; they are the same task. You decide whether each statement matches, contradicts, or is absent from the writer's own opinions and claims.

How It Differs From True / False / Not Given

  • True / False / Not Given tests facts: things that are objectively so, whoever wrote them.
  • Yes / No / Not Given tests the writer's opinions and claims: what this particular author believes or argues.
  • Here, NOT GIVEN means the writer expresses no view on the statement at all, not that a fact is missing.
  • This task only appears with a passage that argues a point of view. Official material says at least one of the three passages contains a detailed logical argument, and this task is set on that kind of text, never on a purely factual one.
  • Write YES, NO or NOT GIVEN. Writing TRUE or FALSE on this task is marked wrong.
⚠ Keep these two things at the front of your mind for the whole task. First, you are marking the writer's opinions, views and claims, not facts about the world. Second, you must write YES, NO or NOT GIVEN. Writing TRUE or FALSE here scores nothing, however good the thinking behind it was.

How to Approach It

  1. Underline words in the statement that show it is about an opinion, not a fact: "believes", "argues", "the real reason", "should".
  2. Scan for the matching part of the passage. In practice these answers usually appear in the order of the passage, but IELTS does not publish this as a rule, so use it as a guide for where to start and never as proof of an answer.
  3. Check whose opinion is being reported. A view the writer only quotes from someone else (an "advocate" or a "critic") is not automatically the writer's own view.
  4. Compare the statement's strength to the writer's: an absolute claim ("always", "never") is NO if the writer only hints at something weaker.
  5. If the writer never states a view on the exact point in the statement, choose NOT GIVEN. Do not guess what they would probably think.
⚠ Trap: a strong opinion reported from someone else is mistaken for the writer's own ⚠ Trap: assuming NOT GIVEN means the writer disagrees, when really they simply never mention it ⚠ Trap: an idea the writer clearly believes is worded so differently in the statement that it looks unrelated
A statement can be factually true in the real world and still be the answer NO, if the writer of this particular passage argues against it. You are marking the writer's claims, not the real world.

Decide What Each Answer Would Look Like, Before You Search

Almost every mark lost here is lost by deciding with a feeling: "hmm, that sounds like what they think." Feelings are not repeatable, so they cannot be trained. Replace the feeling with fifteen seconds of preparation before you go near the passage. Settle all three possibilities first.

  • A YES would look like: the writer saying this statement, in whatever words they choose.
  • A NO would look like: one specific sentence. Write it out. What would the writer have to have written for this statement to be wrong?
  • A NOT GIVEN would look like: the writer circling this subject without ever giving a view on this exact point, or not touching the point at all.

Now go and hunt for the NO sentence specifically, and nothing else. When you have finished looking, decide using these three lines and no others:

  • The writer says the NO sentence you wrote: the answer is NO.
  • The writer says the statement itself: the answer is YES.
  • The writer says neither of those two things: the answer is NOT GIVEN.

Writing the NO sentence first turns a vague search ("is this view in here somewhere?") into a search for one specific thing. You know what you are hunting before you start, so you can stop when you find it and stop when you have proved it is not there.

✓ Write the NO sentence before you search ✓ Hunt for that one sentence only ✓ Let the three lines above choose the answer

What NO Really Covers

NO is not only the flat opposite of the statement. It also covers any claim the passage shows to be inaccurate: the right idea with the wrong strength, the wrong amount, the wrong person, or the wrong reason attached.

If the writer says a few schools have tried a method and the statement says the writer thinks most schools have tried it, that is NO. The passage has shown the statement to be wrong, and that is enough. The same rule applies to FALSE on the True / False / Not Given task.

What NOT GIVEN Really Means

NOT GIVEN means the whole meaning of the statement is missing from the passage. That is the only thing it means.

It does not mean the view sounds unlikely, and it does not mean you think this writer would probably disagree. Predicting what someone would say is not the same as reading what they did say. If you are reasoning about the writer's character instead of reading their sentences, stop and ask one question: is this exact view on the page at all?

Four statements, and the small words that decide them (full worked example)
Passage extract

The present funding model, in my view, cannot hold. Ministers will almost certainly raise the student fee again within the next five years, and I would argue that they should not. The fairer route is to ask employers, who gain most from a trained workforce, to pay directly. Some universities have begun small experiments along these lines, though most still depend entirely on the fee.

Statement 1

"The writer expects ministers to raise the student fee again within five years."

  • A NO would be: the writer saying the fee will not rise, or is unlikely to rise, in that period.
  • What the writer says: "Ministers will almost certainly raise the student fee again within the next five years."
  • Decision: that is the statement itself. The answer is YES.

Statement 2

"The writer believes the student fee should be raised again."

  • A NO would be: the writer saying the fee should not be raised.
  • What the writer says: "I would argue that they should not."
  • Decision: that is the NO sentence exactly. The answer is NO.

The word that decides it: "should" against "will". Statements 1 and 2 are almost the same sentence, and their answers are opposites. "Will" is a prediction about what is going to happen. "Should" is a judgment about what ought to happen. This writer predicts the rise and objects to it in the same breath, which is a perfectly ordinary thing for a person to do and a very common trap in this task.

Statement 3

"The writer says most universities have started charging employers directly."

  • A NO would be: the writer saying that only a few have started, or that most have not.
  • What the writer says: "Some universities have begun small experiments along these lines, though most still depend entirely on the fee."
  • Decision: the writer says the NO sentence, and spells it out twice over. The answer is NO.

The word that decides it: "some" against "most". Swapping one quantity word for another is enough to turn a YES into a NO, even when every other word in the statement matches the passage.

Statement 4

"The writer thinks employers would accept the extra cost."

  • A NO would be: the writer saying that employers would resist, refuse, or object to paying.
  • What the writer says: that employers gain most and ought to pay. Whether they would agree to it is never raised, in any form.
  • Decision: neither sentence is on the page. The answer is NOT GIVEN.

Why it is tempting: someone arguing that employers should pay sounds like someone who thinks they would agree. But that is your reasoning about the writer, not the writer's own claim, and only their claims are being marked.

The Trap List

These statements are built on purpose, and the same dozen or so tricks come round again and again. Learn to recognise them so you know where to read hard. But recognising a trap is not an answer on its own: it tells you where to look, and the three lines of the decision above still decide.

TrapWhat it looks like, in one line
Absolute words
only, all, never, the first		 Writer: teaching is often undervalued. Statement: the writer believes teaching is never valued.
Widened or narrowed scope Writer: criticises one country's housing policy. Statement: the writer criticises housing policy everywhere.
Swapped subject
right opinion, wrong target		 Writer: admires the design of the scheme but doubts the people running it. Statement: the writer admires the people running the scheme.
Whose view it is Writer: reports that critics call the reform rushed. Statement: the writer calls the reform rushed. Reporting a view is not holding it.
Unstated cause Writer: says the scheme was expensive, and separately that it failed. Statement: the writer blames the cost for the failure. Both claims are there, the link between them is not.
Unstated comparison Writer: praises two different schemes in turn. Statement: the writer prefers the second one. The writer never puts them side by side.
A number attached to a different thing Writer: 40 per cent of the budget goes on staff. Statement: the writer says staff costs have risen by 40 per cent.
A belief presented as fact Writer: economists once believed trade would even out. Statement: the writer believes trade evens out. "They believed it" is not "I believe it".
Time expressions Writer: I have opposed this policy since 2015. Statement: the writer opposed the policy for years before 2015. The same year, the opposite decade.
"More than" and "up to" Writer: the reform could save up to a third of the budget. Statement: the writer says it will save a third. "Up to a third" is equally satisfied by a twentieth.
A plan or a hope treated as a fact Writer: hopes schools will adopt the method. Statement: the writer says schools have adopted the method. Wanting it is not reporting it.
A word the writer never touches Writer: argues at length about fairness. Statement: the writer thinks the scheme is too expensive. Cost is simply not in the text. This is the classic NOT GIVEN.
The strength of a small word Writer: the policy might help. Statement: the writer is confident the policy will help. In the same family, "should" is not "will" and "some" is not "most".
⚠ One warning that covers the whole list above. None of these traps carries an automatic answer. An absolute word such as "never" is not proof of NO, and an unfamiliar word is not proof of NOT GIVEN. The writer contradicting a statement and the writer never expressing a view on it are two completely different situations, and only the passage in front of you can tell you which one you are in.
