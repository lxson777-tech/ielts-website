Summary, Note, Table & Flow-Chart Completion

Summary Completion

What is it?

You are given a shortened version of part of the passage with gaps in it, and you fill the gaps. The summary may be laid out as a paragraph, as a set of notes, as a table, or as a flow chart. Officially these are one question type, and they behave identically, so learn them once.

Two Forms

  • Without a word list. You take words directly from the passage, obeying a word limit such as "NO MORE THAN TWO WORDS".
  • With a word list. You choose from a box of options (A, B, C…). Here the words are usually synonyms, not passage words, and there are always more options than gaps.

Key Rules

  • The summary usually covers only one part of the passage, not all of it. Find that part first.
  • Answers to a summary appear in passage order, even though the summary itself is compressed.
  • Never change the form of a word taken from the passage. If the text says migration, do not write migrating.
  • A hyphenated word counts as one word. A number written in digits counts as one word.
  • Exceed the word limit and the answer is marked wrong, even when the meaning is right.

How to Approach It

  1. Read the summary's title and the whole summary through once, ignoring the gaps, so you know what it is about.
  2. Find where the summary starts in the passage. The first gap anchors you.
  3. For each gap, decide the part of speech before you read: does this slot need a noun, a verb, an adjective?
  4. Predict the meaning from the surrounding sentence, then look for the passage's paraphrase of it.
  5. Write the answer, then reread the completed sentence. If it is not grammatical, it is not the answer.
  6. With a word list, cross off options as you use them and never leave a gap blank.
⚠ Trap: copying more words than the limit allows ⚠ Trap: changing singular to plural ⚠ Trap: with a word list, the passage word is often not an option
Passage: The Return of the Beaver

Beavers were hunted to extinction in Britain in the sixteenth century, prized for their fur and for castoreum, a secretion once used in medicine. Their absence went largely unremarked for four hundred years. It was only when hydrologists began modelling flood risk in the 1990s that the scale of what had been lost became apparent: the dams the animals build slow the passage of water through a catchment, holding it back in wet months and releasing it slowly in dry ones.

A trial reintroduction on the River Otter in Devon, monitored for five years, produced results that surprised even its supporters. Peak flows downstream of the beaver dams fell by around thirty per cent. Water leaving the site carried significantly less sediment than water entering it, because the ponds behind each dam act as settling tanks. The pools also proved unexpectedly rich in wildlife, with amphibian numbers roughly tripling over the study period. The chief objection came from farmers, whose fields were sometimes waterlogged where a dam had raised the local water table, and the scheme now includes a compensation fund for exactly this problem.
