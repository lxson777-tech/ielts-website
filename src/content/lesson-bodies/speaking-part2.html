1
Test Format

What is Speaking Part 2?

The Basics

  • Also called the Long Turn or Individual Long Turn
  • You receive a cue card with a topic and bullet points
  • You have 1 minute to prepare: you are given a pencil and paper
  • You then speak on your own for 1 to 2 minutes. The examiner listens and does not join in
  • The examiner stops you at 2 minutes and then asks 1 or 2 short questions about what you said (see Section 6)
  • The whole Part 2 section lasts about 3-4 minutes

What the Cue Card Looks Like

Describe a place you have visited that you found particularly interesting.
You should say:
  • where the place is
  • when you went there
  • what you did there
  • and explain why you found it particularly interesting
You will have one minute to prepare before speaking.

Key Rules

  • You are expected to talk for one to two minutes: stopping after a few seconds leaves the examiner very little to assess and pulls Fluency and Coherence down
  • You do not need a conclusion. The examiner stops you at two minutes, so plan to still be talking when that happens
  • The bullet points are there to help you build the talk, not a checklist you have to tick off in order (see Section 4)
  • You may look at your notes while you speak. Glance down for a word, then look back up at the examiner
  • You can adapt the truth, or borrow a story from someone you know, since your facts are not checked
  • You are marked on the same 4 criteria as Parts 1 and 3
✓ A fluent minute and a half beats a padded two minutes ✓ Making notes during prep time is strongly recommended ⚠ Don't stop after twenty seconds and say "I've finished"
What the examiner can and cannot do in Part 2 The examiner may repeat the instructions if you ask. That is all. They may not explain the cue card, tell you what a word on it means, or suggest ideas for you, because everything you say has to be your own English. This is different from Part 3, where the examiner may rephrase a question for you. In Parts 1 and 2 they may not. So if a word on the card is new to you, work it out yourself (Section 5 shows you how) rather than asking.
2
Preparation

Making the Most of Your 1 Minute

The Four Step Checklist

Run through these four steps every time, in this order. The whole minute has a job to do, so don't spend it all on step one.

  1. Check your subject fits every part of the card, including the time frame. Read the topic line and all the bullets again, not just the first one. If the card says "in your childhood", a holiday you took last year does not fit, however good the story is. A subject that only fits three of the four bullets will leave you stuck halfway through.
  2. Pick the easy subject, not the impressive one. You are marked on your English, not on how interesting your story is. An ordinary trip to your grandmother's village that you can describe in detail will always score higher than a dramatic story you can only half explain.
  3. Plan language, not just ideas. Note down one past memory, one hope for the future, one conditional sentence ("if I went back, I would..."), two topic words you want to use, and one comparison. That is a plan the examiner can actually hear.
  4. Note one backup idea. Write a second angle in two or three words, in case the first one runs out after 40 seconds. A backup is the difference between a short pause and a long silence.

What to Write in Your Notes

Use key words and short phrases. Not full sentences. Your notes are a reminder, not a script. Focus on:

  • A specific person/place/event to talk about (be concrete)
  • An idea or two for the bullets you want to use
  • 1-2 good vocabulary words you want to use
  • One conditional sentence you can say out loud
  • Your backup idea, in two or three words

How to Choose Your Topic

Always choose something specific and concrete, not vague. A specific choice gives you much more to say.

  • ✗ "a place I visited" → ✓ "the Louvre in Paris in 2019"
  • ✗ "a book I read" → ✓ "1984 by George Orwell at age 17"
  • ✗ "a person I admire" → ✓ "my secondary school English teacher, Mr. Ali"

Sample Prep Notes (30 seconds)

Topic: Interesting place → Chichen Itza, Mexico, 2022
Where: Yucatan peninsula, southern Mexico
When: March 2022, family trip
What: climbed pyramid, saw ball court, guided tour
Why: ancient Mayan civilisation. Scale, mystery, unexpected
Key words: awe-inspiring, crumbling, civilisation, remnants
Conditional: if I went back, I'd hire a local guide
Compare: bigger than anything I'd seen at home
Backup: the heat + the market outside
Can I choose a topic I know nothing about? Your facts are not checked, so if nothing in your life fits the card exactly, move to something close that you can describe confidently, and say so in your first line. Keep it close to something real. A talk that has clearly been learned by heart sounds flat, and a talk that has been swapped for a completely different subject can be discounted. Choose a subject that lets you show rich vocabulary and varied grammar.
3
Opening

Your First Sentence

The examiner starts listening the moment you open your mouth, so your first sentence is the cheapest place in the whole test to show good English. Here is the same card opened three different ways.

Describe a book you enjoyed reading.
You should say:
  • what the book is
  • when you read it
  • what it is about
  • and explain why you enjoyed it
You will have one minute to prepare before speaking.
Version 1: copied

"I would like to describe a book I enjoyed reading. The book I enjoyed reading is a book that I read."

This reads the card back word for word. It costs you ten seconds and shows the examiner nothing, because every word in it came from the card and not from you.

Version 2: paraphrased

"I'd like to tell you about a book that I really got a lot out of."

Safe and much better. You have changed the card's words into your own ("enjoyed reading" becomes "got a lot out of"), which is exactly what a paraphrase is. Use this one if you are nervous.

Version 3: upgraded

"The book I want to talk about is a novel called 'The Kite Runner', which I read during my final year at school, about five years ago."

This is the strongest of the three, and it is not harder to say. It swaps the vague word "book" for a precise noun ("a novel"), names it, and adds a time phrase ("during my final year at school, about five years ago"). In one sentence the examiner has heard exact vocabulary, a past tense and a relative clause, and you have already answered two of the four bullets.

✓ Precise noun: novel, documentary, ceremony, colleague, cousin ✓ Time phrase: back in 2019, during my final year, a couple of summers ago ⚠ Never start by reading the card out loud
4
Talk Structure

How to Structure Your 2-Minute Talk

Use the PEEL method as a loose framework. It keeps your talk organised and ensures you cover everything.

P
Point. Introduce Your Topic (15-20 seconds)

State what you're going to talk about clearly. Briefly introduce the who/what/where. "I'd like to talk about…" or "The place I want to describe is…"

E
Explain. Use the Bullet Points to Build the Talk (60-70 seconds)

The bullets are there to help you structure the talk, not to be ticked off in order. You may take them in a different order, spend far longer on one than on another, skip one that does not really fit your subject, and add an angle of your own that is not on the card at all. What matters is that you keep talking about the topic on the card. Give specific details, examples and descriptions.

E
Elaborate. Add Personal Feelings / Reactions (20-25 seconds)

Expand beyond the bullet points with your personal response. How did you feel? What surprised you? What stood out? This is where strong vocabulary shines.

L
Link. Only If You Get There (10-15 seconds)

You do not need a conclusion, because the examiner stops you at two minutes and most people are still mid-sentence. Treat this as a spare part. If you do run dry early, round off with a short reflection ("Looking back…", "What I take from this is…") and then keep going with your backup idea rather than stopping.

Useful Phrases for Each Stage

  • Introduction: "I'd like to talk about…" / "The [topic] I'm going to describe is…" / "This is something I feel strongly about because…"
  • Moving to a new idea: "In terms of [idea]…" / "As far as [idea] is concerned…" / "When it comes to [idea]…"
  • Elaborating: "What really struck me was…" / "I was particularly impressed by…" / "Looking back, I think what made it special was…"
  • If you do reach an ending: "All in all…" / "To sum up…" / "It's definitely an experience I'd recommend because…" (then carry on with another detail)
✓ Use "In terms of…" to move smoothly from one idea to the next ⚠ Don't just list facts. Include feelings and opinions too
5
Rescue

If the Card Doesn't Fit, or You Don't Understand It

Sooner or later you will turn over a card about something that has never happened to you, or that uses a word you have never met. You cannot ask for help (see Section 1), and you cannot swap the card. What you can do is bridge, adapt, and say so.

Two Bridge Sentences

Learn these by heart. They give you five or six seconds of thinking time while still sounding natural, and they are useful on any card.

  • "That's an interesting topic, and the first thing that comes to mind is…"
  • "I've never really thought about this before, but I suppose the best example I can give is…"

Say one of them, then go straight into your subject. Don't say both, and don't repeat them later in the talk.

Adapt the Subject, and Say So in Your First Line

If the exact thing on the card does not exist in your life, move to the nearest thing that does, and tell the examiner you are doing it. Saying it out loud turns a problem into a full, confident sentence.

  • Card asks about a friend, no friend fits: "I'd like to talk about my cousin Aida, because she's honestly the closest thing I have to a best friend."
  • Card asks about a famous person you admire: "I don't follow celebrities much, so instead I'd like to describe a local doctor who's well known in my neighbourhood."
The limit: stay on the card's main topic Adapting means moving to a near neighbour of the subject, not changing the subject. A cousin instead of a friend is fine, because the talk is still about a person you are close to. Talking about your phone when the card asks about a person is not: it looks like a rehearsed answer you have brought into the room, and a talk the examiner believes is memorised can be discounted, which costs you far more than an imperfect answer would have. Whatever you adapt, every sentence should still be recognisably about the topic printed on the card.

If a Word on the Card Is New to You

The examiner is not allowed to define it for you, so work it out yourself in your prep minute:

  • Read the bullet points first. They usually make the topic line clear, because they ask you where, when, who and why about the same thing.
  • Pick the most likely everyday meaning and commit to it. A sensible guess you talk about fluently scores; silence does not.
  • Then say your reading in your first line, so your talk makes sense even if you guessed slightly wrong: "I understand this as a place where people go to relax, so I'd like to describe…"
✓ A confident talk on a slightly wrong reading still shows your English ⚠ Don't ask the examiner what the word means. They cannot tell you
6
After the Talk

The Rounding Off Questions

When your two minutes are up the examiner stops you and asks one or two short questions about what you have just said. These are called the rounding off questions. They close Part 2 rather than open Part 3, and they are the one place in Part 2 where a short answer is exactly right. The long turn is over, so nobody expects another speech.

What They Sound Like

  • "Would you go there again?"
  • "Do you still see this person often?"
  • "Is this a skill your friends have too?"

They always come straight out of your own talk, so you cannot prepare them in advance, and you don't need to.

How to Answer

  • One or two sentences is plenty
  • Answer the question directly, then add a short reason
  • Don't start a second long talk. The examiner will simply move you on to Part 3
  • Don't worry if you only get one question, or none at all
Too short

Examiner: "Would you go there again?"

"Yes."

One word gives the examiner nothing to hear. Even a short answer needs a reason attached.

Just right

Examiner: "Would you go there again?"

"Yes, definitely. I'd like to go back in winter, when there aren't so many tourists around."

Direct answer, one reason, done in eight seconds. That is the right size here.

7
Language

Useful Language for Part 2

Describing Places

  • breathtaking / awe-inspiring / stunning
  • bustling / vibrant / lively / tranquil / serene
  • well-preserved / crumbling / renovated / abandoned
  • off the beaten track / touristy / unspoiled
  • surrounded by / nestled in / perched on / overlooking

Describing People

  • dedicated / passionate / inspiring / compassionate
  • down-to-earth / approachable / outspoken / reserved
  • had a lasting impact on / influenced / shaped
  • looked up to / admired / respected
  • went out of their way to / made a point of

Expressing Feelings & Reactions

  • I was completely taken aback by…
  • It left a lasting impression on me
  • I was moved / overwhelmed / blown away
  • It was quite unlike anything I'd experienced before
  • I hadn't anticipated how… it would be
  • It gave me a real sense of…

Extending Your Talk

  • What made it even more special was…
  • Another aspect worth mentioning is…
  • It's also worth noting that…
  • I think what I'll always remember is…
  • Come to think of it…
  • I suppose what struck me most was…

Practice

Cue Card Practice

How to Practise Read the cue card, take exactly 1 minute to make notes using the four step checklist, then set a timer for 2 minutes and talk until it goes off. Record yourself if possible. After speaking, compare your answer to the model answer below, and ask yourself one rounding off question about what you said.
Describe a memorable journey or trip you have taken.
You should say:
  • where you went
  • who you went with
  • what you did during the trip
  • and explain why the journey was memorable
You will have one minute to prepare before speaking.
Show Model Answer & Key Vocabulary

"I'd like to talk about a trip I took to Istanbul a few years ago with two of my closest friends. It was actually a spontaneous decision (we booked the flights just two weeks before), which made the whole experience feel even more exciting.

In terms of what we did, we spent most of the time exploring the old city. Visiting the Hagia Sophia, wandering through the Grand Bazaar, and taking a boat trip along the Bosphorus. What really struck me was the sheer contrast between the modern city and its ancient history. In the morning we'd be walking through a 1,500-year-old cathedral, and by the afternoon we were sitting in a trendy rooftop cafe overlooking two continents.

Looking back, I think what made the trip so memorable was the spontaneity of it. We had no fixed plan, which meant we stumbled across things we'd never have found on a guided tour. Tiny spice markets, hidden courtyards, local restaurants with incredible food. I was completely taken aback by how welcoming the city was. It's definitely a place I'd return to."


  • Key vocab: spontaneous, sheer contrast, stumbled across, welcoming, trendy, wandering
  • Structure: Opened with a precise noun and a time phrase → worked through the ideas → elaborated with a personal reaction → rounded off without a formal conclusion
  • Grammar used: third conditional ("we'd never have found"), "would" for a hypothetical future ("I'd return"), relative clauses
Describe a person who has had a significant influence on your life.
You should say:
  • who this person is
  • how you know them
  • what this person has done
  • and explain why they have had such a significant influence on you
You will have one minute to prepare before speaking.
Show Model Answer & Key Vocabulary

"The person I'd like to talk about is my secondary school English teacher, whose name was Ms. Kowalska. I first met her when I was around 14 years old, and she taught me for three years.

What set her apart from other teachers was her genuine passion for literature and language. She didn't just teach us grammar rules. She made us fall in love with reading by choosing books that were actually relevant to our lives. I remember she introduced us to George Orwell's '1984', which completely changed the way I thought about language and power.

In terms of her influence on me personally, I think she's the main reason I pursued languages seriously. She had this remarkable ability to make every student feel capable. When I was struggling with writing essays, she stayed after class on multiple occasions just to help me improve. She went completely out of her way for her students.

All in all, I think the greatest gift she gave me was confidence in my own voice. I wouldn't be where I am today without her encouragement."


  • Key vocab: genuine passion, set her apart, went out of her way, remarkable ability, confidence in my own voice
  • Tenses and forms used: past simple, past continuous, and a second conditional ("I wouldn't be where I am today without her encouragement")
Describe a skill you have learned that you consider useful.
You should say:
  • what the skill is
  • how and when you learned it
  • how often you use it
  • and explain why you consider it useful
You will have one minute to prepare before speaking.
Show Model Answer & Key Vocabulary

"The skill I'd like to describe is public speaking. Specifically, the ability to present ideas clearly and confidently in front of an audience. I started developing this skill in my first year at university, when I was required to give presentations as part of my coursework. To be honest, I was absolutely terrified at first.

I improved mainly by doing it repeatedly. Joining a debating club, volunteering to present at team meetings, watching videos of skilled speakers to understand how they structured their arguments and used pauses for effect. It was a slow process, but over time I noticed a real transformation.

These days I use this skill on a fairly regular basis. In work presentations, in negotiations, and even in social situations where I need to express an idea persuasively. What I find most valuable about it is that it's transferable to almost every area of life. In today's world, being able to communicate clearly and confidently is arguably as important as any technical qualification."


  • Key vocab: transferable, persuasively, transformation, arguably, confidently, repeatedly
  • Good phrases: "over time I noticed", "to be honest", "what I find most valuable"
