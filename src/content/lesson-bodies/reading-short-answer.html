Short-answer Questions

What is it?

The official name for this task is Short-answer Questions. On the paper it reads something like: "Answer the questions below. Choose NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER from the passage for each answer." You are asked direct questions about specific facts and must write the answer yourself, using words taken from the passage. It is set on factual, information-dense text, and it can appear with any of the three passages.

Key Rules

  • Never exceed the stated word limit. Read it every time, it changes between tests
  • A hyphenated word such as "space-based" counts as one word
  • Numbers can be written as figures (e.g. "1608") rather than spelled out, and a number counts as one word
  • Contracted words such as "they're" are never tested
  • Spelling must be correct. A correct word spelled wrongly is marked wrong
  • Answers follow the order of the passage
  • If a question asks "how many" or "how much", expect a number in the answer, not a written-out description

How to Approach It

  1. Read the question and underline exactly what it is asking for: a date, a name, a number, a reason, an object.
  2. Identify what part of speech or fact-type the answer must be. This narrows your search fast.
  3. Scan the passage in order for the relevant section. Short-answer questions almost always follow the passage's own sequence.
  4. Lift the exact word(s) that answer the question. Do not paraphrase them into your own words.
  5. Check the word count and the spelling before moving on.
  6. If your answer feels too long, reread the question. There is almost always a shorter, more precise word sitting in the passage.
⚠ Trap: writing more words than the limit allows by adding an unnecessary article or adjective ⚠ Trap: spelling a name or technical term from the passage incorrectly ⚠ Trap: answering with a paraphrase instead of the exact word the passage uses
Worked example: if a question asks "By how many times could Galileo's telescope magnify objects?", the answer "twenty" (or "20") counts as one word, since it is a single number written either way. If a question asks what kind of instruments modern observatories rely on, the answer "space-based" counts as one word too, even though it contains a hyphen, so it still fits inside a one-word limit.
Example passage: The Development of the Telescope

The first telescope for which a clear historical record exists was made in the Netherlands in 1608 by an eyeglass maker named Hans Lippershey. His device used two lenses fitted inside a tube and could magnify distant objects by about three times. Word of the invention spread quickly across Europe, and within a year the Italian scientist Galileo Galilei had built his own improved version, achieving a magnification of around twenty times.

Galileo was the first person to turn a telescope towards the night sky in a systematic way. In 1610, he observed four moons orbiting Jupiter, mountains and craters on the surface of the Moon, and countless stars too faint to see with the naked eye. These discoveries directly challenged the widely held belief that the Earth stood motionless at the centre of the universe, since Jupiter's moons proved that not everything in the sky revolved around the Earth.

For the next two centuries, astronomers worked to build ever larger refracting telescopes, which use a curved glass lens to gather light. This approach had a natural limit, however, because very large lenses tend to sag under their own weight and distort the image. In 1668, Isaac Newton solved this problem by building the first practical reflecting telescope, which used a curved mirror instead of a lens. Modern observatories, including space-based instruments, still rely on mirrors rather than lenses for exactly this reason.
