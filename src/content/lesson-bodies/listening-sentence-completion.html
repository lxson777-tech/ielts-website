Sentence Completion

What is it?

  • The official name is Sentence Completion
  • The instruction line reads something like: "Complete the sentences below. Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS for each answer."
  • You are given a sentence with one gap, summarising a point from the recording, and you complete it with the missing word or short phrase
  • Answer format: the exact word(s) you hear, within the stated limit
  • Appears most often in Part 4, and sometimes in Part 3

Key Rules

  • The completed sentence must be grammatically correct. This alone rules out many wrong guesses before you even check the meaning
  • Words come directly from the recording, not a paraphrase you invent yourself
  • Answers follow the order of the sentences on the page
  • Spelling and word limit are both marked strictly, exactly as in every other completion task
  • A number counts as one word and a hyphenated word counts as one word, so "well-known" fits a one-word limit

How to Approach It

  1. In the pause before the recording, read each sentence and decide what part of speech the gap needs: a noun, a verb, an adjective, a number.
  2. Use that same pause to predict roughly what the missing idea might be, based on the rest of the sentence.
  3. Listen in the order of the sentences, since the lecture or talk moves through the same points in the same sequence.
  4. When you hear content that matches the sentence's meaning, check silently that the exact word you plan to write completes the sentence grammatically.
  5. Write the word you actually heard, not a synonym of it, and move on the instant you have it.
⚠ Trap: the word you hear does not fit the sentence grammatically unless you change its form slightly, so listen for the exact form used ⚠ Trap: a tempting word appears just before the true answer, as a distractor ⚠ Trap: writing a synonym instead of the word actually spoken
Grammar is your best clue Before you even think about meaning, look at what comes immediately before and after the gap. If the gap follows "the", you almost certainly need a noun. If it follows "to", you likely need a verb. This narrows the field of possible answers dramatically, often before you have heard a single word of the recording.
Transcript. Read at a steady pace, as if listening. (Simulation: Lecture Extract on Sleep and Memory)

Lecturer: This morning I want to look at why sleep matters so much for memory. For years, researchers assumed sleep was simply a period of rest, but we now understand it as an active process during which the brain reorganises what it has learned. During deep sleep, the hippocampus, which stores short-term memories, appears to replay the events of the day, transferring the most important details to the cortex for long-term storage. Interestingly, this replay happens faster than the original experience, sometimes twenty times faster. Sleep deprivation studies make the stakes very clear: students who stayed awake all night before a memory test performed significantly worse than those who slept normally, even when both groups studied the same material for the same length of time. What's more, it isn't only the total number of hours that counts. The proportion of time spent in deep sleep, rather than lighter stages, predicts performance most strongly. This is why naps, although useful, rarely match a full night's sleep for consolidating complex information. One practical implication is that cramming through the night before an exam is almost always counterproductive, since it sacrifices exactly the process the brain needs to lock the material in.
