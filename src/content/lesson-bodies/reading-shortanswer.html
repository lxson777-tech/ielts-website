Short-Answer Questions

What is it?

You answer direct questions about factual detail in the passage, using words taken from the passage itself, within a stated word limit. The questions are ordinary questions: who, what, where, when, how many, how long.

Key Rules

  • Obey the word limit exactly. "NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER" means three words is fine, four is wrong.
  • Use the passage's own words. Do not paraphrase your answer.
  • Do not write a full sentence. The answer is the missing information, nothing more.
  • Answers appear in passage order.
  • Articles (a, the) count towards the word limit, so leave them out unless they are needed.
The easiest marks in the paper, and the easiest to throw away This is the most mechanical question type in IELTS Reading: the information is stated plainly and you copy it. Nearly every lost mark here comes from breaking the word limit, misspelling the answer, or answering in a full sentence. Read the instruction line before you read the questions, and write the limit at the top of your page.

How to Approach It

  1. Read the instruction line and note the exact word limit.
  2. Read the question and decide what kind of answer it needs: a name? a date? a number? a material?
  3. Underline the keyword and scan the passage for it or its paraphrase.
  4. Read the sentence around the match, and take the shortest phrase that answers the question.
  5. Count your words. Then check your spelling, because a misspelled answer scores zero.
⚠ Trap: answering in a sentence ("It was made of oak") ⚠ Trap: exceeding the limit by including "the" ⚠ Trap: paraphrasing instead of copying
Passage: The Longest Migration

The Arctic tern makes the longest annual migration of any animal on earth. Birds tagged in Greenland in 2010 were found to cover around 71,000 kilometres in a single year, flying from their Arctic breeding grounds to the Antarctic pack ice and back again. Because the tern follows the northern summer south into the southern summer, it experiences more daylight over its lifetime than any other creature.

The route is not direct. Rather than crossing the Atlantic in a straight line, the birds trace a wide S-shaped path that allows them to ride prevailing wind systems, a detour that adds thousands of kilometres but saves a great deal of effort. Many pause for roughly one month in the North Atlantic to feed on zooplankton before continuing. Terns are long-lived by the standards of small birds, commonly reaching thirty years of age, which means a single individual may travel some two million kilometres in a lifetime. Roughly the distance of three return trips to the moon.
