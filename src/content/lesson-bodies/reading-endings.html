Matching Sentence Endings

What is it?

You are given the first half of several sentences, each based on the passage, and a longer list of possible endings labelled A, B, C… You choose the ending that correctly completes each sentence according to the passage. There are always more endings than questions, so some are never used.

Key Rules

  • The completed sentence must be true according to the passage, not merely grammatical.
  • It must also be grammatically correct. Grammar eliminates options fast.
  • Each ending is used once at most.
  • The sentence beginnings follow passage order. The endings do not.

How to Approach It

  1. Read the sentence beginnings first. They tell you which parts of the passage matter and in what order.
  2. Underline the keyword in each beginning, then locate that idea in the passage.
  3. Read the passage carefully and predict your own ending before looking at the options.
  4. Now find the option that matches your prediction. This stops you being pulled around by attractive wording.
  5. Use grammar to eliminate: if the beginning ends with "the researchers", an option starting with a plural verb may fit but a singular one cannot.
  6. Do the ones you are sure of first. Every ending you use removes an option from the others.
⚠ Trap: an ending that is true but attaches to the wrong beginning ⚠ Trap: an ending that repeats passage words but reverses the meaning ⚠ Trap: choosing on grammar alone. It must also be factually right
Passage: Why Cities Are Getting Hotter

A city is reliably warmer than the countryside around it, an effect first documented in London in 1818 by the chemist Luke Howard and known ever since as the urban heat island. The mechanism is unglamorous. Brick, asphalt and concrete absorb solar radiation during the day and release it slowly through the night, so cities never cool down fully before the sun rises again. On a still, clear summer night the gap between a city centre and its surrounding fields can reach eight degrees.

Vegetation works in the opposite direction. Trees shade surfaces before they can absorb heat, and they cool the air directly by transpiration, releasing water vapour through their leaves. A mature street tree has roughly the cooling effect of several domestic air-conditioning units, and unlike an air conditioner it does not vent hot air into the street. This is why the cheapest municipal response to rising summer temperatures is almost always planting rather than engineering. Singapore has pursued this strategy for four decades and now claims more tree canopy per resident than it had in 1986, despite a near doubling of its population.
