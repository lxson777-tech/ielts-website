Short-answer Questions

What is it?

  • The official name is Short-answer Questions
  • The instruction line reads something like: "Answer the questions below. Write NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS AND/OR A NUMBER for each answer."
  • You are asked a direct question (who, what, where, how many, how much) and you write a short factual answer from the recording
  • Answer format: a short phrase, name or number, within the stated word limit
  • Appears most often in Part 1, and sometimes in Part 3

Key Rules

  • The word limit applies strictly, and a hyphenated word counts as a single word ("self-guided" is one word, not two)
  • Figures are allowed for numbers, so "4" is accepted just as readily as "four" unless told otherwise, and a number counts as one word against the limit
  • Answer using words taken directly from the recording, not your own paraphrase
  • Answers follow the order of the questions

How to Approach It

  1. In the pause before the recording, read every question and underline the question word (who, when, how many), since that tells you exactly what kind of fact to listen for.
  2. Predict, where you can, the type of answer: a place name, a number, a time, a single word.
  3. Listen for the question being answered in the order it was asked, and write the answer as soon as you hear it rather than waiting for the sentence to finish.
  4. Count your words against the limit before moving on. A hyphenated word such as "self-guided" or "well-lit" counts as one word only.
  5. Where a number is involved, writing the figure ("4") is just as acceptable as the word ("four"), so don't waste time deciding between them.
⚠ Trap: writing two or three words when a single hyphenated word from the recording was all that was needed ⚠ Trap: answering the wrong question because two questions ask about similar-sounding details ⚠ Trap: giving a vague answer ("some") instead of the specific fact stated
Short answers stay short Resist the urge to write a full sentence. If the question asks "How many languages is the tour available in?", the answer is "four", or "4" if you prefer, but only one of them, not "the tour is available in four languages". Extra words risk pushing you over the limit for no benefit.

Writing your answer

More marks are lost on the form of an answer than on understanding the recording. You hear the right detail, you know it is the right detail, and then you write it down in a way that cannot be accepted. Two habits fix nearly all of it: predict the word type in the pause before you listen, then follow the answer rules when you write.

Half 1. Predict the word type before you listen

  • The question carries the same clues a gap would. If it prints "a" or "the" in front of the missing item ("What is the name of the ___ the group uses?"), the answer is a noun, and "a" means it must be a countable singular noun, so an uncountable word such as "equipment" or "clothing" is impossible there.
  • The question word itself fixes the shape. "How many" needs a number. "Where" needs a place, not a person. "When" needs a time or a day. "What are members asked to do" needs a verb, not a thing. A preposition in the question narrows it further still.
  • Turn the question into a statement, put your answer inside it, and read it back in your head before the next question comes. If it does not sound like English, it is wrong, however clearly you heard the words.

Worked example: grammar alone decides it

Recording: "On Saturdays the walkers meet their guide by the old boathouse, and then set off along the river path."

Question 5 (NO MORE THAN THREE WORDS): "Where do the walkers meet on Saturday mornings?"

✗ Looks right, marked wrong
their guide

By meaning it is tempting, because the speaker says the walkers meet their guide, and those are the words right next to "meet". But "where" has to be answered by a place, and "the walkers meet at their guide" is not English.

✓ Correct
old boathouse

Read it back: "The walkers meet by the old boathouse." It is a place, so it fits the question word, and it is two words.
